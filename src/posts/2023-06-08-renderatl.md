## Day 2

### Aisha Blake
Another leadership session. Aisha took a novel approach to the topic using a Game Master (GM) analogy. This assumes most of the audience were familiar with table top games. I suspect we were.

She expressed the challenges of leading a team through chaos, like a D&D campaign and share her own experiences doing this very thing with a team that had many unforeseeable problem. She made the point of creating an atmosphere where feedback between you and your team would work even if power dynamics were at play (you're the GM after all).

Core needs were organized with the acronym B.I.C.E.P.T.S., Belonging, Improvement, Choice, Equality, Predictability and Significance. Basically, take care to include, be fair to, empower and establish a sense of purpose with the members of your campaign.

She then listed a number of adversaries you're team may encounter as you make you're way across the map. Uncertainty, burnout, disruptive AI and conflict, and the different effects (damage points?) it may have on certain team members over others (damage resistance?).

It was a really clever analogy now that I think about it.

## Rizel Scarlet
Github's Copilot has been on my radar (har har) for some time now but I've never given it a try. Rizel's presentation has me really interested now though.

She demonstrated how Copilot can be used like an autocomplete feature in email applications - only with code. But it's more sophisticated than that. Copilot uses an AI that is trained on "millions" of lines of code. It can suggest entire functions, as well as a selection of options, based on comments you type directly into your IDE.

In order for it to work, you'll need a paid subscription and, in the case of VSCode for example, the Copilot extension installed.

Rizel noted that this tool is not simply copying and pasting code from the web (like you do from Stack Overflow), but synthesizing generative suggestions. From Copilot's website, its powered by _"Codex, a generative pretrained AI model created by OpenAI. It has been trained on natural language text and source code from publicly available sources, including code in public repositories on GitHub"_

Regarding security, Rizel insisted that there were safeguards in place to prevent vulnerabilities from making there way into suggestions. But Copilot' website is careful to warn users that it is their responsibility to review suggestions manually.

A short list of advantages to using this tool include: few trips to Google (who does that regex go?), suggestions based on your patterns, cleaner code, multilingual translations and auto-generation of documentation.

She concluded with a convincing demo where she created an API powered app that auto posted a tweet to her account. This took her little more than a minute - and she showed us the tween on her Twitter profile. Powerful stuff.

### Scott Tolinski
I didn't recognize Scott's name at first. But quickly realized who he was when the podcast he co-hosts, Syntax, was mentioned in his intro. I don't listen to Syntax as much as I listen to Shoptalk but maybe other people do. Scott had a bigger cluster of fans around him prior to his talk than Chris Coyier did.

In any case, much like Chris's talk, Scott was here to talk about new CSS features. And because of this there was some overlap. I didn't mind this as this was going to help me remember these things better.
Aside from that Scott went about it in a slightly different way. He'd mention the new feature, describe it and then give it a grade of "ship it", "try it" or "check it", based on full, partial or little browser support, respectively. He also added when a feature could be used with the help of CSS post processing.

Notable in both Scott and Chris's presentations, Firefox was frequently the culprit when it came to lagging browser support for new CSS features.
